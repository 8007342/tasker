# Do not assume an icon is universal; test it with the workers

@trace spec:research-records

## Claim

Farm workers and low-literate readers regularly misread pictograms, so comprehension
has to be tested with the people who will use them.

## Evidence

**Seen only through search summaries of abstracts; neither paper could be
opened, so strength is capped at expert-opinion.**

- Rother 2008: 115 vineyard workers, Western Cape, 10 common pesticide-label
  pictograms. 50% or more gave misleading, incorrect or critically confused
  readings, often reflecting social and cultural frames rather than the intended
  message. Authorship comes from one listing, not checked further.
- Dowse & Ehlers 2004: 23 international and 23 local pictograms, 304 low-literate
  respondents, eight language groups. Education affected interpretation; cultural
  differences among the African language groups were not significant. One search
  summary gave 14% to 97% correct per pictogram and 2 of 23 international versus
  12 of 23 local reaching an 85% criterion; a second search could not confirm
  these figures, so do not cite them from this record. The repository link is the
  search hit for this work; it could not be opened to confirm the exact item.
- ISO 9186 uses a roughly 67% correct-comprehension criterion, as quoted in
  secondary sources; the standard itself was not read.

Hazard pictograms convey abstract risks. Concrete objects like a watering can may
fare better, but none of this evidence tests that; it is an inference.

## How it applies

- As per research:usability/pictograms-need-local-testing-dowse-rother, test each
  task icon by showing it to several workers and asking what it means, before it
  ships.
- Prefer objects and actions workers handle daily over abstract symbols, a design
  choice these sources support only indirectly.
- Treat the seed icon and green play button in the spec as hypotheses until tested.
